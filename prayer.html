Prayer to the Imperfect

Oh boyo, this is going to take a few hot hours to write. I watched the movie 'Frankenstein', 2025 by Guillermo del Toro last night. I'm moved for sure. I'd like to get the obvious out before I start. It's a 4.5 or 4.8 for me, if not a whole 5-star watch for me. The cinematography and the imagery is just perfect and really well in tone with the theme of the movie and its many messages. It's vivid and spectacularly beautiful. Jacob Elordi did an amazing job playing a "monster." It felt natural asf, although his chin gets me each time---for the good, lol. It's definitely out there on my top 10 movies of all time if I stretch it a bit. But for sure, one of the best movies to come out in 2025.

I must warn myself and the reader---if anyone's reading at all---that unfortunately I haven't read the original text/book yet. I would've done it today if I wasn't already reading a pretty heavy book at the time of writing this essay. So, I will just focus on the movie. And I'm aware that things have been tweaked to make it more entertaining in the movie. However, in the middle of watching the movie (not having read the book), my mind totally went on a really hard swing from "ooh, how are they going to kill it and cool action scenes?" to the theological and more aspect of it (at least how I see it). What I'm going to think about here is how and why I think this entire idea or movie is just seraphic in an imperfect and broken way.

I'm going to talk less or not at all about the "ooh, scary monster amalgamation---how do I kill it?" aspect but rather more about the whole divinity of the topic and its portrayal. I will try to stick with the movie and not force the text into believing that it's the original book's realization manifesting, but if I do sway away in that direction regardless, then forgive me. But yeah, that's the gist of my goal with this. I felt like I had to get it out no matter how claustrophobic it ends up sounding or chaotic or not at all, but yeah.

For an overall idea: with men abandoned in the Arctic ice on their ships and ending up rescuing our doctor Victor there and fighting our beloved Franks (Frankenstein), is how the story starts off. The entirety of the story is told in this time in present as Victor remembers the past and tells the captain of the ship his story---his side of the story from the past to the present, which involves the conception of the idea of, and thus the creation of Franks. When Franks finally breaks into the ship and finds the captain and Victor, he starts explaining his side of the story. After that, they finally end up on the same page. For my idea, I will talk about Victor here as 'God' in meaning, while Frankenstein represents 'creation'. I'll try to bring the idea out of my head the best i can. Okay, no more boring stuff!

We start with God (Vic), as shown, still in his formative years. He has a whole world he exists in. His decisions impact him and those around him. He is "taught" by his "father" to be just like him---to be the best in what he does and to live up to the reputation of being a "God." Victor fails at both. He fails to become the best doctor of his time, like his father, or in any sense similar. If his father was exemplary, why is Victor not so? Is it because Victor is an entity in itself? Or is it because Victor is perfection embodied when what "perfect" means is broken? Anyways, he carries on with his own studies; he focuses on bringing back the dead and ending death completely. That's one of the many jobs of being a god, no? Giving life and ensuring it lives a happy life free of worries, free will etc. But Victor, like we know now, is imperfect. If I would've latched on to this early enough, I would've thought about what happens when God himself is NOT flawless---what will his creation be like? Victor focuses a lot on giving life to his creation, and never are we told about what he means to do with what he creates. This question isn't answered until that time truly arrives. We must know how Victor, despite not knowing answers to a lot of his own questions, wants to give birth to life. Jumpy. Since he knows no further objectives beyond the idea of birthing, it is safe to say that he is doing it out of his own incredible curiosity, and that is the case too! On that instance- when God wants to create for no further reason, for his own "curiosity," just to show that it CAN be done, blind to consequences---on that instance, instead of giving birth to a beautiful answer, what we get is another equation. One meant to remain unsolved- one that knows not why it is here, because its creator did not either. But the creation doesn't know that its god is unknown to answers.

All Franks knows is to exist, to be alive, and to somehow find meaning in a world that it was brought into by someone who is not a perfect god. He's not a perfect creation. He is born out of curiosity and a play of ego and not out of meaning or resolve- into an imperfect world. And after all, when God knows not the answer to the world, then it's only a matter of how hard its creation can bang its head on the altar until it realizes so too- and gives up. Gives up to do what? Just existing? Creating its own meaning in the world? Or is life itself an answer to some question that is already out there, answered by the mere existence of 'life'? In the movie, we see a god that is curious, in love, a little cheeky, and unfortunately scared of his own creation.

Amid Franks coming to life and Victor conceiving the idea, the act with Elizabeth is important. Victor is infatuated by Beth, but it never truly plays out. She is a person (as depicted) with a deeper understanding of things beyond science than Victor is. She understands the human spirit and love and how small things really matter and even make the biggest difference. Victor is alien to all of this. And we see it play out after Franks is alive. When Franks finally comes to life, Victor is overjoyed---his 'experiments' indeed paid out, and the result is here, 'alive' and breathing. He tries to teach him language and tries to understand him, only for a little while. Franks here is like a baby. All he knows right now is his creator's name. "Victor"; however, Victor gets incredibly impatient with Franks. As if he had a goal anyways? He said it himself, he didn't know what to do with him. Is that the true nature of God? Do we also exist for no thought out or well presumed, planned out reason? Just a food for thought.

Victor gets mad at Franks for not being intelligent and eventually hurts and tries to kill Franks because he thinks Franks is dangerous, when all he did was react to the pain Victor was inflicting on him in the guise of "lessons." Here, we see what it meant to be a God like Victor. Or really, I should say: if you could, you'd understand how it felt like to be creation itself. You're expected to be intelligent and to be fast and adaptive; otherwise, you will be punished. This is not just an idea but somewhat real too. Take natural selection for example, survival of the fittest. But is this the ONLY universal idea life is to go by? But is that what the nature of things really should be? Any lesson we take right now is incomplete because it's already established that Victor is not a perfect god, we should not forget this, and the things he does---he doesn't know the meaning of himself. Unfortunately, Franks will always be at the receiving end of this half-knowledge that Victor holds. He now has to build his own philosophy and intelligence, and survive by his own little wisdom in an unforgiving world. After trying to kill Franks, Victor runs off and vanishes. God has abandoned its creation.

What is it that we learn from, or at least try to gather from, this act is what bothers me a little, because it could either make perfect sense to someone who has suffered in real life, while it could also sound messed up or chaotic to someone who hasn't. When suffering is prolonged and, more importantly, when it is true, then that state of being becomes the true norm, even if so for a phase. Acceptance seems like the only viable alternative if you don't want to break down completely in such situations. It just so happens that this constant flux of thoughts trying to find an answer, an explanation, just any sort of meaning in suffering---this whole process burns a part of you off. Maybe permanently. And it hurts. When you know something's out there, some bigger power that's supposed to look after you, and you beg for it again and again to step in, but your prayers just feel like they're hitting a void, it hurts. One could either continue to hold on to faith or break apart and be free from the pain of asking and not ever receiving. But is giving up faith really an option? You know it all too well that someone IS out there, something bigger---call it luck, the universe, God---and it has a hand and a role in everything you were, you are, and will be a part of. I know all too well that I am going to itch forever for some warmth in any form, but all I get is silence---a temporary or a really long reality I'll have to get accustomed to---is just how it Is no? Is it a good option to not be alive- or in a bigger sense, to go looking for answers beyond life? Does that truly solve it? What if the prayers have already been answered? What if the answer to them is to just live? No matter how imperfect the world we were sent to is---what if it itself IS the answer? It was made for us. All of it for us to experience and to find meaning in. Is it possible to look away from the sky and shout out our prayers into the world itself? But how can someone who's broken, someone so wretched, ever be the same creation that once was when it wasn't so miserable---when God hadn't left it to fend for itself? It changed. But did it change for the better or for the worse? And if it changed for the worse, and the worse was caused by lack of meaning, then is the creation really to blame? Who is it but God to give meaning? And if I am his creation, then who am I but a part of God---a part of his conception, a part of his brain and thinking? Must I look for answers within myself or in this world made for me to investigate? If I must look into the world as the answer and still not find it and break my heart over it, then I will stand at fault myself. But if you're not, then do you have what it takes? Do you even have the guts to blame God? Do you have the guts to give a cold shoulder to a colder shoulder?

In the second act of the movie, Franks comes into the ship and explains his side of the coin. How he was never a dangerous beast. How he was never at fault for things he is held accountable for. His story begins with surviving the attempt on his life by his creator. He flees to a land quite far away and meets other forms of life and, unfortunately, other humans who shoot at him and try to kill him because he is a beast. He runs and hides into a hidden room in a house of a local family. The father, mother, daughter, and an old man make that up. This is the beginning of his new life. He knows too little to wander outside. He stays in there, hidden from them all, and observes them. Through this, he learns. He learns to speak, to interact, discovers what a family is, which is also something he longs to be a part of. He tries to help the family out in a discreet manner by helping them in their daily lives. He gathers hella wood, builds fences and stuff for them, and the family thinks that the spirit of the forest is helping them out. The old man here is really the focus.

Dare I say I want to make him God number 2? But he's really just an angel here. When the family moves out, he makes himself known to the old man, who accepts him as his friend, showing nothing but love and acceptance. He teaches him, reads books with him, and the most important detail in all of this was that the man was blind. Any man at the sight of Franks would most likely freak the fuck out because he is full of scars, tall and is frightening. But that's his outer appearance. The old man sees not the physicality but the intentions---and only intentions did Franks have in his heart. This makes the old man's judgment real and honest. In Franks, he finds a wandering soul that is super lost. He finds a man that is cold to the bone and doesn't know warmth yet. The old man is kind to him. Really, for me, the old man depicts an honest friend; finding whom was a matter of sheer good chance for our baby Franks here. So, I suppose that's one good thing that happened to him. The old man implores him to remember anything he can and go towards what he thinks gives him his meaning. Franks remembers the word "Victor," which was the only word he kept screaming in panic as Victor tried to kill him. Franks reaches the old fort where he was created by Victor and finds out the true meaning of his being. He realizes that he is not a true man (but it doesn't matter, tbh). He is an experiment with no meaning. He cannot die either, so that's another final bliss stolen from him by Victor.

His god gave him nothing. He searches for Victor not in rage but as in a search for God. He knows that his god is the only one who can do something. Upon meeting when he is told he can't be helped and Victor turns away, he is incredibly enraged---and, oh boy, rightfully so. All he asked for was a companion to spend the rest of his life with, but Victor denies him that for the most fuck-all reason.

There is so much meaning here. It's showing us what happens when God has forsaken its creation and is terrified of it despite the creation being inherently harmless. Terrified? Embarrassed? Scared of being held answerable to flaws in himself by his OWN creation? Being hit with the reality that he was never a real God?

A lot of running around happens when Victor, once again, tries to end it once and for all and tries to kill Franks again (I hate him so much). He fails, of course, because he is a powerless god, confused and a terrible one. He knows not how to give or take. In the process of trying to blow Franks up with a bomb, which he fails at, he gets injured heavily and runs away and is taken into the ship by those sailors. And later, soon, Franks enters the ship too and finds him again, bringing us to the present.

We could do a little thought experiment ourselves if we want to grasp the unease of it. When you sleep tonight and close your eyes, I want you to imagine your God closing her eyes from you. Be it God, your luck, your charm, whatever is the biggest force driving you. Do you feel scared? Or do you feel rather hollow? It is, I think, the subtlest but most meaningful of a distinction. It is necessity and reliance against fulfillment and want, and somehow either is strong enough to change entire courses of lives. Franks puts his hand on Victor's heart in the ship, on the bed, kisses his creator's forehead, and forgives him.

Cold abandonment and search for answers, while having the ability to feel pain, is a most terrible suffering, and it stings so bad. Franks tried searching for answers. When he realized there wasn't one, he looked for meaning in his existence---to find someone to spend his wretched life with, at the very least. He was robbed of that also. What does a soul so broken have to lose anymore? He's out of prayers. You're out of prayers. But he knows that despite this utter misfortune, he is alive. And his creator, as a last wish, asks Franks to be alive, to live and exist. Franks is not the same baby he was anymore. He has gone through loss and through rage. The old man and his lessons were nothing but life playing itself out. A part of his heart that he was born with has been metaphorically burnt off in this churning of the soul. Sure, he isn't the same person he was anymore, but now he is one with acceptance. Sure, he might never get the explanations he wanted, but he, for now, chooses to look the other way too, which isn't behind but just next to the path of even more continued suffering followed by intrinsic meaning followed by healing. No matter which path he takes, he will live on with a heart that has had its fair share of heat. It will hurt---maybe every day if it isn't fixed, (if it can be fixed anyways)---but at least he is alive to see the end of his suffering, which is one last favor he can do for himself.

This story was never about a good ending and acceptance. It was about acknowledging your pain, realizing your suffering, and trying to become the next version of yourself, whatever it is---with the suffering in your mind as nothing but an imprint of the lessons you've been through. Maybe by living for himself, he could now find solace. One thing's for sure, and that is that our Franks will no longer suffer more again; rather, just continue with half a heart that learns to rebuild itself through life. The story is unfortunate, and there's no happy ending to it---until acceptance is your bliss. For I know something all too well, and that is when you're in pain and you've begged relentlessly for it to stop and revert or just get normal, it's like dragging yourself on sandpaper. Even if in the end it all goes back to normal, that new normal will not be lived in by a person that wished for it, for he is dead. And if it doesn't, then living on and acceptance is a bitter medicine for those willing to be subjectively strong enough to take it.
